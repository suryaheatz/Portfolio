Reimagining the HMI for SAE Level 4 autonomous cars

ZF's engineering team had been building a physical control knob — the Centronic, later called MMA (Multimodal Armrest). The knob could change its rotational resistance in real time according to the UI — for eg. volume up/down will have low resistance, drive mode changes will have more resistance.

Centronic ACP demonstrator — the MRF knob, gear selector, and ambient light surface paired with the UI tablet

At Level 4, the old automotive HMI constraints disappear. You can look at the screen, lean back, use both hands. The UI has to be effective to show the hardware capabilities.

The question handed to our team was: what does the software experience look like?

Case study

    The Context

    Starting point

    The armrest had no language.

    In a mid-size passenger car, the centre armrest sits between driver and passenger — physically central, yet entirely passive. No controls. No feedback. No interface. The Centronic brief was to change that: design an interaction model for a new tactile input device that could live in that space and let the passenger control cabin functions without lifting their eyes off the road — or the conversation.

    The Research

    Start–Stop–Continue matrix — mapping what cabin controls passengers actually use, and how often
    Competitive IA audit — how five OEMs structure dashboard information: BMW · Mercedes · Audi · GM · Tesla

    Two lenses shaped the design: a frequency-vs-importance matrix of existing cabin interactions (what people reach for, how often, and what they skip), and a competitive audit of how five OEMs structure their dashboard information architecture. The pattern was consistent — music, climate, and navigation were the three functions passengers wanted most, but the existing interfaces all required visual attention to operate.

    The Scenario

    Gesture vocabulary

    Six domains, one thumb.

    The MRF (Magneto-Rheological Fluid) thumbwheel responds to pressure, rotation, and directional swipes — making it possible to encode six cabin control domains into a gesture vocabulary a passenger can learn in a single ride. The mindmap defined the mapping: which gesture activates which domain, and how the ring resistance changes to signal context.

    The Hardware

    Production-intent demonstrator — MRF thumbwheel + embedded display + ambient light layer
    Expo build — V2 UI running on the full demonstrator stand
    Modular config — tablet screen + standalone controller puck, portable for client demos

    Wireframes & Gesture Mapping

    Main menu — 5 modules (Music · Nav · Phone · Menu · Climate) arranged as a radial bubble cluster
    Climate sub-screen — 7 zone bubbles; size encodes recency of use
    Phone sub-screen — 4 nodes, gesture-navigable without looking at the display

    These are the V1 shipped screens — the redlined production specs handed to the engineering team. The bubble UI maps directly to the thumbwheel's gesture vocabulary: rotate to orbit the cluster, press to select, swipe to jump between domains. No text labels required for navigation.

    The Design Evolution

    Pre-workshop concept (draft 02) — first attempt at mapping swipe gestures to the pill-shaped controller form
    Draft 03 — single-tap + swipe-right gesture variants explored before the workshop aligned the team
    V2 main menu — tablet-first approach with a richer visual language; not shipped due to build timeline
    V2 climate sub-screen — dynamic bubble sizing by zone priority; complex but visually coherent

    The design moved through three distinct phases. Pre-workshop drafts established the gesture grammar. The 3-day workshop locked the interaction model and the bubble UI metaphor. V2 extended that metaphor onto a full tablet with richer visual hierarchy — but the engineering team couldn't build it in the given timeline, so V1 (the armrest-native bubble UI) shipped as the final product.

    The Interaction Model

    "One thumb, zero eyes. The system has to be fully navigable by rotation and pressure alone — the passenger should never need to look down at the controller to operate it."

    — Guiding principle · ZF Centronic interaction model, 2019

    "Every gesture teaches itself. The first time you rotate the wheel into a new module, the ring resistance changes. That tactile boundary is the tutorial — no onboarding screen needed."

    — MRF haptic design principle · ZF Centronic, 2019

    The interaction model was built around a single constraint: the passenger must never need to look at the device. Every function in the system maps to a gesture — rotate to browse, press to select, swipe to jump domains. The MRF ring changes resistance at module boundaries, giving the passenger a physical sense of the menu structure. The display exists as confirmation, not as the primary input signal.

    The Ambient Language

    Haptic layer

    Light as the last channel.

    Beyond the screen and the thumbwheel, the Centronic communicates through light. Ambient LEDs under the fabric surface of the armrest pulse and shift colour in response to module state — a slow breath for idle, a warm amber when music is active, a cool blue for navigation. The passenger doesn't need to read the display to know the system is listening. The armrest tells them.

    Where the industry went

    The haptic rotary control we prototyped in 2019 is now showing up everywhere. BMW's iDrive in the i7, the Mercedes S-Class center console, Volkswagen's Golf — all moving toward physical controls with variable feedback, after a decade of touchscreen-only cabins.

    Euro NCAP's 2026 mandate requires physical volume and navigation controls — exactly the case we were making internally. ZF productized the Centronic MRF technology under the name HaptiX.

    None of these can be attributed directly to this project — supply chain documentation doesn't work that way. But the direction we argued for in 2019 is now industry standard in 2024.
