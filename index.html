Case study — Reimagining the HMI for SAE Level 4 autonomous cars.

SAE Level 4 means the car is driving. The person sitting in front is no longer a driver — they're a passenger who happens to be in the driver's seat. I was a Senior UX Designer at ZF's India Technology Centre when this brief came through: design a physical HMI for a cabin where driving is no longer the primary task.

Centronic ACP demonstrator — the MRF knob, gear selector, and ambient light surface paired with the UI tablet

The brief

ZF's engineering team had been building a physical control knob — the Centronic, later called MMA (Multimodal Armrest) — with magnetorheological fluid inside it. The knob could change its rotational resistance in real time, zone by zone. That was the hardware. The question handed to our team was: what does the software experience look like?

The existing automotive HMI paradigms — list menus, nested grids, iDrive-style rails — were designed for a driver whose eyes had to stay on the road. At Level 4, that constraint disappears. You can look at the screen. You can lean back. You can use two hands. The design space opened up completely, and the instinct to copy what existed felt wrong.

We started with a workshop instead of a wireframe.

Climate module, V1 — zone-based bubbles map directly to cabin areas. Knob resistance increases as you approach edges of a thermal zone.

Where the industry went

The haptic rotary control we prototyped in 2019 is now showing up everywhere. BMW's iDrive in the i7, the Mercedes S-Class center console, Volkswagen's Golf — all moving toward physical controls with variable feedback, after a decade of touchscreen-only cabins.

Euro NCAP's 2026 mandate requires physical volume and navigation controls — exactly the case we were making internally. ZF productized the Centronic MRF technology under the name HaptiX.

None of these can be attributed directly to this project — supply chain documentation doesn't work that way. But the direction we argued for in 2019 is now industry standard in 2024.
